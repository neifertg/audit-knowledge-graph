**#operating-model.**

> **What the rounds measured.** Cypher needed less query in rounds [1, 6 and 7]: with the same number of stages on both sides, SQL needed more joins than Cypher needed hops, to fetch a name or to join a grouped result back. The languages came out close in rounds [3 and 4], where SQL's joins were no more than Cypher's hops, and in round [2], where Cypher needed an extra stage. SQL needed less query in round [5], the question of exposure through fourth parties, of unknown depth: asked on its own, the question was shorter in Cypher, [12] against [19], but bringing the new register in cost the graph [29] against SQL's [10], because a new table had to become nodes and edges.
>
> **Where a graph should earn its place.** *(expected)* When auditors explore rather than report, as in clicking through a blast radius; when the same entities are linked from many systems; when new relationship types keep arriving and the data is already in the graph; and at larger scale, which is the M2 benchmark's question.
>
> **When good SQL views are enough.** The measured rounds say: when the joins a question needs are no more than the hops, and when new data arrives as tables. *(expected)* Most audit and data teams already read SQL, which matters as much as query length.
>
> **Keys for success.** *(practice)* Reconcile first: a graph of unreconciled data only draws the confusion more vividly. Keep the graph an analysis layer over the system of record, not a replacement. Prove both stores hold the same facts on every load. Keep a human review queue for uncertain matches. Publish every answer with its checks.
>
> **Maintenance** (a table). *(practice)* A taxonomy change: re-run the mapping, clear the review queue, regenerate. A new source system: map it into both stores and extend the parity test. A change to the matching rules: retrain on the training bank and rescore. A new planning question: write it in both languages with an oracle and an agreement test.
